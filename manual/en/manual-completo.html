iPrivacy

PollinationPro runs entirely inside your browser. Your presence records, your visit observations and your climate layers are read on your computer and are never sent to any server. Only when you ask for it does the program query two public services —GBIF, for the names and the records, and a public database of biotic interactions— and whatever they return stays on your machine. Everything else works without the internet.

The illustrations, diagrams and screenshots in this manual are original: they were generated with the program itself or with code written for the manual. All the results shown come from the practice study included with the program —“Cucurbits and squash bees”— and from its practice environmental layers. Both are fictitious: the taxon names are real so that the example reads like a study, but no point, date, visit count or climate value reproduces an observation, and the app says so every time they are loaded. I.1What is PollinationPro?

PollinationPro is a platform for taking a study of plants and their flower visitors from occurrence records to the paper: it gathers the GBIF records, the interactions already published and your own observations; it cleans them and harmonizes their taxonomy; it puts them on a map; it measures whether the plant and the visitor coincide in space more than chance predicts, whether they fly and flower in the same months and at the same elevation, whether they share the same climate and where the plant could be left without any potential visitor, today and with future climate; it analyzes the network of recorded visits; and it delivers a report with figures, methods written out and a package that reproduces it.

It works for any crop —vegetables, fruit trees, coffee, cocoa, cucurbits, oilseeds—, for its wild relatives and for any native plant, with any group of visitors: bees, bumblebees, stingless bees, flies, butterflies, bats or birds. Everything happens inside the browser: there is nothing to install and no need to know how to program. It is written for botanists, entomologists and thesis students who know that most records of a crop are of planted individuals, that bee taxonomy changes, that records pile up where there are roads, and that a reviewer will strike out the sentence “it is its pollinator because their distributions overlap”.

I.3Five ideas behind the manual

You do not need to be a specialist in pollination ecology to use the app, but five ideas come back again and again. Understanding them turns the results from numbers into arguments.

1. Coinciding is not pollinating

For an insect to pollinate a plant it has to be where the plant is, when the plant flowers, in environments both tolerate; it has to visit the flower and, in doing so, carry and deposit compatible pollen. Presence records inform the first three links; visit observations, the fourth; the fifth can only be proven in the field. That is why Blocks 5 to 8 speak of opportunity for interaction and of potential pollinators, and only Block 9 speaks of interactions. A paper with occurrence data is valuable precisely because it narrows down where, when and in what climate the field evidence is worth looking for.

2. The record reflects the recorder

GBIF records pile up near roads, cities, universities and research stations. If the plant and the bee were recorded more where more sampling took place, their records will coincide even if in nature they do not seek each other. Every test in the app that compares an observed value with an expected one does so within the target group: the cells, the months or the environmental background where someone recorded something from the study, which are the ones that were really sampled. The first lab of Block 1 shows, with a landscape whose truth is known, what happens when this is not done.

3. The year is a circle

December 31 and January 1 are one day apart, not 364. A species that flowers from November to February has its peak in December–January, not in June as the arithmetic mean of the months would say. That is why dates are read with circular statistics: each date is an angle, the mean date is the direction of the average vector, and its length R̄, from 0 to 1, says how concentrated the season is.

4. The niche is compared in the environment, not on the map

Two species can live in different places and share a climate, or live close by and use different environments. Block 7 compares them in environmental space —the first two principal components of the region’s climatic variables—, corrects for what the environment offers and asks whether the niches are identical (equivalency) or more alike than the mere likeness of the available environments would produce (similarity). Block 8 takes that suitability back to the map.

5. The visit is the evidence, and sampling has an end

The network of Block 9 is built not from coincidences but from observed visits: yours or published ones. And a network is always incomplete: how many visitors remain unrecorded is estimated with the rarefaction curve and with Chao1, and completeness tells you whether to go back to the field before claiming that a plant has few visitors.

I.4Data it accepts and what it delivers

What goes in

  • A study list: the scientific names of the plants and the visitors, at any level (species, genus or family), typed by hand or taken from one of the seven example systems. Each name is a unit of analysis.
  • GBIF records, downloaded from the app by name and by country, only with coordinates, with filters for years, type of record and geospatial issues.
  • Published interactions from a public database of biotic interactions, searched in both directions (the visitors of each plant and the plants of each visitor).
  • Your presence records —one row per plant or insect seen, with taxon, latitude and longitude— and your visit observations —one row per plant–visitor pair with how many visits—, from .csv, .txt, .tsv, .xlsx or text pasted from a spreadsheet.
  • Environmental layers in GeoTIFF (.tif) or ASCII grid (.asc), in degrees, for Blocks 7 and 8, and the same variables for a future scenario.
  • A project saved earlier as .json: the list, the records, the interactions and the visits, as they left Block 2.

What comes out

  • On-screen tables with reading scales: next to almost every number there is a label —moderate, marked, acceptable, generalist— with the scale that justifies it one click away.
  • Figures in every block, each with its ⤓ button to export it and its ✎ button to edit its texts, colors and sizes; and the map with its own studio.
  • .csv files of every important table: raw, cleaned and removed records with the reason, map points, co-occurrence pairs, circular statistics, phenological pairs, environmental values, mismatch classes, network matrix and species.
  • The report: a single .html file that opens in any browser and prints to PDF, with one section per calculated block, the methods written from what was actually run, the references for those methods, the GBIF datasets that must be cited and the calculation record.
  • The package: a .zip with the report, the project, the parameters, the tables and the figures. Whoever receives it can redo the study.

I.5What you need and how to open it

Online, without downloading anything: open luisangelbg.github.io/PollinationPro in the browser; it is also in the catalog of the LABG Suite (luisangelbg.github.io). Everything is calculated on your computer just as in the local copy: your data are not uploaded anywhere.

On your computer: a computer with a recent browser —Edge, Chrome, Firefox or Safari from the last two or three years— and the program folder. There is no installation: copy the folder and open index.html with a double click. It works from a USB stick and, except for the GBIF download and the search for published interactions, without the internet.

  1. Copy the whole PollinationPro folder to your computer. Inside it there must be index.html and the folders js, css, data (the outlines of the countries and of the states of Mexico) and tests.
  2. Open index.html with a double click. The first screen is Block 1, the home page. On Windows, Open PollinationPro.bat also opens it as a small local server (port 9750), useful for opening it from another machine on the network.
  3. Choose the language with ES · EN and the light or dark theme with the sun button. Both are remembered for next time.
  4. If you want to check that the engine calculates correctly in your browser, open tests/index.html: there are 191 tests against published values, exact arithmetic, formulas solved by hand, independent implementations and simulations under the null hypothesis. They must all come out green.
iThe app remembers the study

The list, the records, the interactions and the visits are kept in the browser of that computer (in its local database), so that when you open the app again it shows “The previous session was recovered”. The options of each block, the map style and the changes made to each figure are also remembered. This is only a convenience: the real backup is the .json project of Block 2, which opens on any other machine. The environmental layers are not kept: they are chosen again in Block 7.

I.8How to work through a study

The order of the blocks is the order in which a pollination paper with occurrence data is written. You do not have to do all of it: a study of distribution and phenology ends at Block 6; one of a visit network needs Blocks 2 and 9. Whatever a block needs from another it calculates on its own when you open it.

  1. Block 2. Build the study list, resolve the names in GBIF, choose the countries, count and download. Search for the published interactions and import your records and your visits. Save the project.
  2. Block 3. Review the fifteen rules, decide plant by plant what to do with the cultivated ones, set the study area, confirm the synonyms and the unit of analysis, and apply. Copy the sentence for the methods.
  3. Block 4. Look at the map: a point in the sea or in the wrong country stands out at once and sends you back to Block 3. Build the figure for the paper and export it.
  4. Blocks 5 and 6. Read the matrix of pairs and the sensitivity to cell size; then the phenogram, the phenological pairs and elevation. Note which pairs coincide in space and in time.
  5. Blocks 7 and 8. Load your layers, calculate the environmental space and the tests; fit the suitability models, check their AUC and TSS, read the mismatch and, if you have future layers, how it changes.
  6. Block 9. With your visits and the published ones, read the network against the null models, the key visitors and the completeness of each plant.
  7. Block 10. Go through the state of the study, export the figures, check the report in the preview and download the package.
!What is upstream rules what is downstream

If you change the list or the records in Block 2, the cleaning is redone. If you change a rule, a synonym or the unit of analysis in Block 3 and apply again, everything below is recalculated when you open it —the map, the pairs, the calendars, the environmental space—. The report of Block 10 is written with whatever exists at that moment: that is why it is best generated at the end, and generated again if anything was touched.

I.9What the program does not do

  • It does not prove that a visitor pollinates. It measures opportunity for interaction and analyzes recorded visits. Pollen load, deposition after a single visit and fruit set are proven in the field, and the program says so in every interpretation guide.
  • It does not include real climate layers. Climate databases do not allow their files to be redistributed: they are downloaded once from their website and chosen in Block 7. The practice layers are invented and are only for learning.
  • It does not replace the official GBIF download for the final paper. The download from the app is for exploring; GBIF asks for every download to be cited with its DOI, which is obtained by repeating it on its portal with the same filters. The report lists the datasets that contributed records and reminds you of this.
  • It is not a full species distribution modeling program. The three models of Block 8 are simple and transparent, designed to compare the plant with its visitors by the same method; a study centered on the distribution of one species deserves a specialized program and more variables.
  • It does not correct taxonomy on its own. It resolves the names against the GBIF taxonomic backbone and applies the synonym rules that you confirm; the decision about which name is correct still belongs to the specialist.
  • It does not decide for you. The decision rules and the scales are guides for reading a number, not verdicts. No warning blocks the work: it points the issue out, explains what it means and leaves the decision to whoever knows the species.

1.3Lab 1: co-occurrence and sampling bias

The first lab answers the question that is most often got wrong in studies with occurrence data: do the plant and the bee coincide more than expected? It generates a landscape of 36 × 24 cells with an environmental gradient. The plant and the bee occupy the cells that suit them according to their optimum and their niche breadth. Then someone samples, closer to a road the higher the bias, and only the sampled cells yield records. Veech’s exact test (Chapter 5) is run on those records with three different universes, and compared with the truth: the same test with the real presences, which in the field are never known.

ControlWhat it changes
Niche separationHow different the environmental optima of the plant and the bee are, from 0 (the same) to 0.8. With 0 they share the landscape; with 0.8 they avoid each other.
Niche breadthHow much of the gradient they tolerate. Large breadths make almost everything coincide.
Bias towards the roadHow strongly sampling concentrates along the road, from 0 to 100%.

↻ Another landscapeDraws another landscape with the same controls. With the same controls and the same landscape, the result is always the same.

The tiles underneath are the lesson. Records and Shared cells give what was observed —45 plant records and 64 bee records, 2 shared cells in Figure 1.2— and how many would be expected with two of the universes. The truth gives what happens in the landscape: the two species avoid each other (“less than expected”, SES −11.12). The next three tiles are the same test with three universes: the whole map calls it “at random” and gets it wrong (in red); the target group —the 155 cells where the sampler recorded the plant, the bee or another taxon of the same group— and the sampled cells recover the segregation (in green). The paragraph below sums it up: “Bias also hides: in the landscape the two species avoid each other, but since both were recorded along the road, with the whole map as the universe the segregation vanishes.”

▸Worked example · bias also invents

Set the niche separation to 0.20, the breadth to 0.25 and the background effort to 5%, with the bias at 80%. In the landscape, the plant and the bee are distributed at random with respect to each other (SES −0.37). But their records pile up along the road, and with the whole map as the universe the test declares a huge positive association —54 shared cells against 9.3 expected, SES 16.38— that does not exist. With the target group (SES −1.68) and with the sampled cells (SES −0.86) it comes out “at random” again. This is the bias trap, and it is the most common mistake in papers with occurrence data.

Now raise the background effort to 100% and lower the bias to 0: the whole landscape is sampled and the whole-map universe is right. The target group, on the other hand, leans towards “less than expected” (SES −2.36), because it leaves out the cells where nobody recorded anything and, with them, the shared absences. The app explains it in the same paragraph: the target group corrects bias, and when there is no bias it costs a little accuracy.

✓Decision rule · which universe to use
If your records come from GBIF or from collectionsthen use the target group (the default option in Block 5): your records carry the road bias.
If your records come from systematic sampling (a grid or transects walked evenly)then the universe is the cells you walked; “all land” is only correct if you sampled all the land.

The ? sheet on Sampling bias explains it with references (Phillips et al. 2009; Kramer-Schadt et al. 2013).

3.1Why clean

A single record at the geographic center of Mexico —the place written down when only the country is known— can create a co-occurrence between two species that have never been seen together. A backyard chayote in Mexico City stretches the climatic niche of the species toward places where it never lived in the wild. And a bee recorded as Peponapis in one collection and as Xenoglossa in another splits into two units that do not recognize each other. Cleaning is not a formality: it is the first scientific decision of the study.

The app applies the checks on which the record-cleaning literature agrees (Chapman 2005; Zizka et al. 2020) and keeps every decision in view, with its count. Nothing is applied until you press Clean and apply, and everything can be redone: the raw records of Block 2 are not touched.

3.2.1 Two definitions of “center”

The place written down when only the country or the state is known is not always the same. Some databases use the area centroid of the polygon; others, the center of the bounding box. The “center of Mexico” that circulates as a placeholder, at 23.6345° N and 102.5528° W, is the second one, and it lies tens of kilometers from the first. The app computes both for every country in the world and for each of the 32 Mexican states from its own outlines, and the rule reaches a record if it falls near either of them.

3.3Cultivated plants and study area

Most records of a crop are of planted individuals, whose distribution reflects agriculture and not the natural niche. What to do with them depends on the question: if you study the crop as it is grown (coffee in its farms), keep its cultivated records; if you study the wild populations or the wild relatives, remove them. That is why each plant is decided separately, and the app proposes keeping them when more than half of that plant’s records are cultivated.

A record is considered cultivated if its establishment means is “managed”, “cultivated”, “introduced” or “planted”, or if its locality or its notes say cultivated, sown, planted, orchard, vegetable garden, garden, backyard, nursery, greenhouse, market, plot, fruit orchard, milpa, coffee farm, plantation, ornamental or escaped from cultivation, in Spanish or English. The column Living collection counts separately the specimens from botanical gardens and field genebanks, which are handled by the rule “Fossils and living collections”.

!Cultivated records without a label are not detected

The rule only sees what the record says. A garden chayote recorded without notes passes as wild. With widely grown crops, check on the map of Block 4 the points in cities and in farming areas, and consider narrowing the study area to the known natural distribution.

The study area. Records outside the area are removed if the rule “Outside the study area” is active. The Countries selector offers those of the download (Block 2) —the usual choice— or all (no area). Choose Mexican states… opens the list of the 32 states: if you choose some, the area is only those. 5.1More than chance predicts?

That the plant and the visitor have records in the same places says nothing unless you know how many co-occurrences would be expected by chance. The test of this block compares the observed with the expected, and the answer depends on two decisions: among which sites chance is spread (the universe) and at what scale you look (the cell size).

θTheory in brief · Veech’s exact test

The territory is cut into cells. If the universe has N cells, the plant occupies N₁ and the visitor N₂, and they were placed at random with respect to each other, the number of shared cells j would follow the hypergeometric distribution. The app computes the probability of observing as many or more (positive association) and as many or fewer (negative association), with no simulations or normality assumptions (Veech 2013). The standardized effect size (SES) says how many standard deviations the observed lies from the expected; |SES| > 1.96 corresponds roughly to p < 0.05 (Gotelli 2000).

P(j) = C(N₁, j) · C(N − N₁, N₂ − j) / C(N, N₂) E[j] = N₁·N₂ / N SES = (j − E[j]) / √Var[j]

The default universe is the target group: the cells where any unit of the study was recorded, which are the ones that were really sampled (Phillips et al. 2009). The alternative, all the land in the extent, also spreads chance over cells nobody visited; the lab of Block 1 (Section 1.3) shows the error that produces.

5.5The overlap of ranges

The fourth card draws the convex hull of each unit —the smallest polygon without indentations that encloses all its points (Andrew 1979)— and computes its area on the sphere (Chamberlain and Duquette 2007), that of their intersection (Sutherland and Hodgman 1974) and what fraction of the plant’s area is covered by the visitor’s.

It is the coarsest reading of the three: a convex hull includes everything lying between the extreme points —in Figure 5.5, a good part of the Gulf of Mexico—, so it exaggerates the area of species with scattered records. It serves to say whether the visitor covers, at least in extent, the area of the plant; not to say whether they coincide within it.

6.1The year is a circle

December 31 and January 1 are one day apart. Treating dates as numbers from 1 to 365 gives a plant that flowers from November to February its “mean” in June. Circular statistics turn each date into an angle and solve the problem (Batschelet 1981; Fisher 1993; Morellato et al. 2010).

θTheory in brief · mean date, R̄ and the two tests

Day d is equivalent to the angle θ = 2π(d − ½)/365. The mean date is the direction of the average vector of all the records, and its length R̄, from 0 to 1, measures concentration: 1 if all fall on the same day, 0 if they are spread evenly over the year. The circular standard deviation, in days, is its more intuitive version. The Rayleigh test says whether the distribution is seasonal (small p) or uniform; the Watson–Williams test, whether two calendars have the same mean date (Zar 2010), and it requires that they not be too spread out (κ ≥ 1).

C̄ = Σcos θᵢ / n S̄ = Σsin θᵢ / n R̄ = √(C̄² + S̄²) θ̄ = atan2(S̄, C̄) Z = n·R̄²

A dated record does not necessarily say that the plant was in flower: a herbarium specimen may be vegetative or in fruit. That is why the checkbox Plants: only records with flowers uses only the records whose reproductive condition says flower or anthesis. And the number of records per month also reflects when people go to the field: the checkbox Correct by the monthly effort of the target group divides each profile by the records of the whole study in that month (Section 6.2).

7.1Comparing climates, not places

Two species may live in different places and share a climate, or live close together and use different environments. For a pollination study, the second matters: if the visitor tolerates a narrower temperature range than the plant, there will be parts of the plant’s range where the visitor cannot live, even if today they share a region. And climate change moves each species according to its own niche.

θTheory in brief · the method of Broennimann et al. (2012)

1) The background cells —the available environment— are summarized with a principal component analysis of the standardized variables. 2) The records of each unit are projected onto the first two axes. 3) On a 100 × 100 grid of that plane, the density of the records and that of the background are estimated with a Gaussian kernel; the density of the records is kept only where there is available environment and, if requested, divided by that of the background. 4) Schoener’s D and Warren’s I compare the densities of the two units, from 0 (niches with nothing in common) to 1 (identical). 5) The equivalency test shuffles the records at random between the two units; the similarity test moves the visitor’s niche to a random place in the background; both are repeated many times (Warren et al. 2008).

7.2The environmental layers

The block works with your layers: GeoTIFF (.tif) or ASCII grids (.asc), in degrees, preferably all at the same resolution. You pick them with Choose files… or, more conveniently, Choose a folder…. TestQuestionChayote × Xenoglossa
EquivalencyAre the two niches identical? If the observed D is smaller than almost all those of the random shuffles (p < 0.05), they are not equivalent.p = 0.01 · the niches are NOT equivalent

▸Worked example · how to write it up

“In the space of the first two principal components of the environment (98.1% of the variance), the niche of Sechium edule overlapped moderately with that of Xenoglossa (D = 0.41; I = 0.65). The equivalency test rejected that the niches were identical (p = 0.01), and the similarity test did not indicate that they were more alike than expected from the available environments (p = 0.27; 100 replicates each; Warren et al. 2008; Broennimann et al. 2012).”

Again: these are the numbers of the practice layers, useful for learning how to write it up.

✓Decision rule · the two tests together
If equivalency p < 0.05 and similarity p < 0.05then the niches are not identical, but they are more alike than the environments explain: they share preferences.
If equivalency p < 0.05 and similarity p ≥ 0.05then the niches differ and their resemblance goes no further than that of the environments: each uses the climate in its own way (the case of the example).
If equivalency p ≥ 0.05then you cannot reject that the niches are identical. With few records the test has little power: say how many there were.

8.1Where the plant might be left alone

Block 7 compared the niches in environmental space; this block takes them back to the map. With a suitability model for the plant and another for each potential visitor, every cell falls into one of four classes: suitable for both, for the plant only, for the visitor only or for neither. The cells for the plant only are the mismatch: the part of the plant’s range that would depend on other visitors, or that would be left without pollination if the relationship were exclusive (Schweiger et al. 2008). Under a future climate, the mismatch may grow or shrink (Kerr et al. 2015).

!This block uses the layers of Block 7

The variables are the ones ticked in Block 7 (they are listed at the top of the card). If there are not at least two layers loaded, the block asks you to go and get them. In this chapter they are the practice layers —BIO1, BIO4 and BIO12—, and everything said about their maps is about an invented climate.

9.1From coincidence to visit

Blocks 5 to 8 measured the opportunity for interaction. This block works with the fourth link of the chain of evidence (Section 1.7): the visit to the flower. A visit network is a matrix of plants (rows) by visitors (columns) with the number of visits observed in each cell, or with a 1 when all that is known is that the link exists.

Pollination networks have a characteristic structure: they are nested —specialists visit subsets of the plants that generalists visit (Bascompte et al. 2003)—, more or less modular —groups of plants and visitors that interact more among themselves— and they have a measurable specialization. Each index is compared with what a random network with the same totals would give: a number without a null model does not say whether the structure is remarkable.

IndexWhat it measuresIn the practice study

▸Worked example · reading the practice network

The practice network is small and highly connected (0.818): almost every visitor reaches almost every plant. Its nestedness (68.8) is no different from that of a random network with the same totals (p = 0.26). Its specialization is low in absolute terms (H₂′ = 0.153, generalist), but much higher than that of chance (z = 34.7): the visitors distribute their visits with preferences, albeit weak ones. And it has three modules: the squash bees, with Halictus, Polistes and Toxomerus, together with Cucurbita pepo and S. mexicanum; the stingless bees Trigona and Partamona with S. chinantlense and S. compositum; and the honey bee, Scaptotrigona and the bumblebee with chayote and S. hintonii.

✓Decision rule · what to report about the network
If the network is small (fewer than 10 species per side)then connectance will be high and nestedness hardly informative; report H₂′, the modules and the sampling completeness before NODF.
If an index does not differ from the null modelthen the structure it measures is not remarkable given the totals: do not discuss it as a trait of the network.

Each term is followed, in italics, by its name in the Spanish interface when it differs.

TermMeaning
AUCArea under the ROC curve: the probability that a cell with a record receives higher suitability than a random background cell.
Background (fondo)The cells of available environment against which what the species use is compared.
Centroid (centroide)The point assigned when only the country or the state is known: the area centroid or the center of the bounding box.
Chao1Estimator of the minimum number of species (here, of visitors) from those seen once and twice.

Clip (recorte)Drawing the map only inside the outline of Mexico or of the chosen states.
Co-occurrence (coocurrencia)Two taxa having records at the same site (cell). It is an opportunity for interaction, not an interaction.
Completeness (completitud)Recorded visitors divided by estimated visitors: how much of the sampling has already been done.
Connectance (conectancia)Observed links divided by possible links of a network.
Convex hull (envolvente convexa)The smallest polygon without indentations that encloses all the points of a unit.
Cross-validation (validación cruzada)Evaluating a model with records that were not used to fit it.
d′Specialization of a species with respect to the availability of its partners, from 0 to 1.
Environmental space (espacio ambiental)The plane of the first two principal components of the climate variables of the background.
GBIFGlobal Biodiversity Information Facility: the portal that gathers occurrence records from collections and observations around the world.
H₂′Specialization of a whole quantitative network, from 0 to 1.
List entry (entrada de la lista)Each name of the study list (species, genus or family), with its role.
Living collection (colección viva)A specimen from a botanical garden or a field genebank; it does not say where the wild population lives.
Mismatch (desajuste)Area suitable for the plant and for no potential visitor.
Modularity Q (modularidad Q)How much a network divides into groups that interact more among themselves.
NODFMeasure of the nestedness of a matrix, from 0 to 100.
Null model (modelo nulo)The random version of the data that keeps what is not in question (the totals, the geography of sampling) and breaks only what is being tested.
Omission (omisión)Fraction of validation records that fall outside the suitable area.
Opportunity for interaction (oportunidad de interacción)Coincidence in space, time and environment: a necessary, not sufficient, condition for pollination.
Potential pollinator (polinizador potencial)A visitor that coincides with the plant in space, time or environment, but whose visits and pollen transport have not been documented.
Published interaction (interacción publicada)A plant–visitor pair recorded in the literature or in collections and gathered by a database of biotic interactions.
R̄Length of the mean vector of the dates: their concentration, from 0 to 1.
Rayleigh test (prueba de Rayleigh)Test that a set of dates is seasonal and not uniform over the year.
Role (papel)Plant or visitor.
Sampling bias (sesgo de muestreo)The uneven distribution of recording effort: near roads, cities and stations.
Schoener’s D (D de Schoener)Overlap of two probability distributions (monthly, elevational or in environmental space), from 0 to 1.
SES (EEF)Standardized effect size: (observed − expected) / null standard deviation.
Suitability (idoneidad)How much the environment of a cell resembles that of the records of a unit, according to a model.
Synonym (sinónimo)An earlier name of the same taxon; the app gathers them under the current name and keeps the original.
Target group (grupo objetivo)The sites (or months) where any taxon of the study was recorded: the universe that corrects for sampling bias.
Threshold (umbral)The suitability value from which a cell is declared suitable.
TSSSensitivity + specificity − 1 of a binary map, from −1 to 1.
Unit of analysis (unidad de análisis)What Blocks 4 to 8 compare: a list entry or a species.
Universe (universo)The set of sites among which chance is distributed in a co-occurrence test.
Veech test (prueba de Veech)Exact hypergeometric test of the number of sites shared by two taxa.
Watson–Williams test (prueba de Watson–Williams)Test that two sets of dates have the same mean date.
